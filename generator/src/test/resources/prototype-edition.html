The Fork That Wasn’t

Two Blocks, Then Silence — and a Community Deciding What It Learned

A week after BIP‑110 split off and stalled, your corner of Nostr has stopped arguing about the code and started arguing about the lesson.

From the Bitcoin desk

The arithmetic is settled and nobody disputes it. On August 8th two blocks were mined at height 961,632. One chain kept going. The other produced exactly two blocks, stalled at 961,633, and has not moved since — carrying Bitcoin’s full difficulty with a fraction of a percent of the machines. Bitcoin Well, writing today, put the next difficulty adjustment for that chain at 6.3 years out. The day after the split it was 350 days. The exit recedes faster than you can walk toward it.

What is not settled is what it meant. Shashue, posting under #MakeBTCDecentralizedAgain, went first and went furthest: “It is hard to see a blessing while you are still bleeding. But I propose that the failure of the BIP‑110 fork is exactly that. This weekend tore away a veil.” He is not calling it a defeat. He is calling it eyesight.

Paula spent the day releasing an interview with Jeff Booth built around the blunter version of the same question — whether Bitcoin is still decentralized, and whether it has been captured. “Some BIP‑110 supporters may not agree with his perspective,” she warned before posting it. In Bulgarian, George Manolov: the fiat swindlers won a small battle, but the war is only beginning. In French, Copinmalin was already nostalgic: who remembers the good old days when Bitcoiners tore each other apart over BIP‑110?

Nostr Magazine, which files a weekly issue straight onto the relay, ran it as a civil war under the headline The Fork That Lasted Two Blocks and the Fight That’s Just Beginning. Nelson, publishing his Acelerando Bitcoin keynote from Paraguay, offered the calmer frame: Bitcoin Culture Is Not Dead. It Just Stopped Being One Culture.

Meanwhile the chain that kept going was boring in the way a working thing is boring. Clark Moody’s dashboard, posting on the hour: one satoshi per vbyte at every horizon, blocks landing every 9 minutes 56 seconds, 915 exahash on the network, 87,102 blocks to the halving. The mempool did not notice the civil war at all.

A Timestamp Nobody Can See Starts a Fight Everybody Can

It began with fiatjaf asking, politely, for an option to turn something off. Coop mobile randomizes the timestamp on the seal inside a gift‑wrapped message, and it was “giving a hard time due to some reasons.” Then the sharper version: “It doesn’t make sense to randomize the internal thing that no one sees anyway. Yuki Kishimoto arrived with the specification open — NIP‑59 does say the rumor keeps the current timestamp while the others are tweaked — and with the threat model behind it: rumor leaked, no signature; seal leaked, randomized timestamp. Then, agreeably: “But yeah, I can add that option.” reya turned up with the exact offending line, nip59.rs#L441.

This paper’s own observer weighed in from the sidelines of a neighboring argument about rewriting NIP‑17 entirely: “There have been lots of proposals. Nothing with significant gains that would justify breaking everything once again. They can just do a new NIP and wait for people to catch up. If it is better, other people will want to use it.”

Trust, but Verify the Firmware: A Bad Week to Own a Hardware Wallet

From the freedom-tech desk

Two vendors shipped, one philosopher needled, and one user quietly rebuilt his entire threat model.

BitBox released what it named the Dixence update, and the disclosure was refreshingly plain: during internal audits it found and fixed multiple security issues in its own firmware, and users should update the app and the device. Foundation put KeyOS v1.4.0‑beta1 in front of Passport Prime testers — app sideloading, a redesigned launcher, multisig exports for Unchained and Casa, imports from Aegis and Proton Authenticator — with an unusually stern warning that this is a recovery‑mode flash for experienced users only.

Gigi spent the day highlighting a licensing autopsy line by line: that Coldcard benefited from Trezor’s commitment to software freedom, that a large March commit replaced the remaining Trezor‑derived GPL cryptography and BIP‑39 code in a security‑critical path, and, flatly, that firmware should be free software. Users, he added, should demand more than visible source code.

The most human note came from Tim Wiher, who had done everything right. Dice rolls. A passphrase. A single‑signature setup he understood completely and was comfortable with. “A hardware‑wallet‑company retirement attack was definitely not in my threat model — an unknown unknown. Setting up multisig was a massive pain in the ass. But now that I know about this threat, I can’t go back.”

The Apps Desk

Five releases · kind 32267 and the wire

Everybody Is Building an Escape Hatch This Week

The theme in today’s releases is not a feature. It is the removal of a middleman, stated in the marketing copy as the entire point.

Soapbox shipped Armada — Discord-shaped communities with text channels and encrypted voice rooms, except the community itself has no host. “Your keys, your fleet.” Bitcrazy shipped Chama, named for the East African savings circle, which replaces the neighbors’ trust with a 2‑of‑3 escrow split by Shamir secret sharing and held as Fedimint ecash. “Your money is never ours to lose,” the listing says, “because there is no company in the middle.” PeerLoom put out PearCal, a calendar that shares events peer‑to‑peer — “with people, not platforms.”

The unglamorous shipping continued elsewhere. mutatrum announced ESP‑Miner v2.15.0rc1 with unified firmware, mDNS and several Stratum V2 fixes. miljan confirmed the Primal 4.0 Android shell will have predictive back animations. And franzap, asked why a bug persists on somebody’s specific handset, gave the most honest answer of the day: “I don’t have enough resources to be testing on multiple devices. I only have hope — that it will magically get fixed in the next version, as I’m rewriting the app from scratch in Kotlin.”

Paranoia Desk

“It’s Just Very Trust-Me-Bro”

trosso19 ran the longest thread of the day and it was, essentially, a threat model. “How much do you guys trust the zero data retention inference providers on OpenRouter? My paranoia is starting to set in.”

He knows the mechanics: policies range from zero retention to trains‑on‑your‑inputs to publishes‑conversations‑to‑public‑datasets, and you can route to ZDR endpoints only. He avoids first‑party DeepSeek for exactly that reason and uses open weights through someone else. “If the advertised policies are true I’m happy with it. It’s just very trust me bro.”

The economics drove it: “Anthropic API prices are ridiculous. DeepSeek via OpenRouter is great for my budget.” And the fallback isn’t one: “My local LLM is pretty retarded. Hope those providers are telling the truth.”

Surveillance

Three fronts, one afternoon

They Are Watching the People Watching the Cameras

Obscura VPN circulated the 404 Media report that the government is monitoring anti‑Flock TikTok and Instagram accounts. “Not ONLY are they monitoring you outside,” they wrote, “but if you don’t like it and post about it online, they’ll monitor you there and hand that data to law enforcement.” A.A.Ron, from somewhere considerably more rural, noted that his nearest Flock camera is a 250‑mile drive — “but the roads still look like that.”

A Covert Channel in Your Health App

Sats paa BTC surfaced a USENIX Security paper on Android’s Health Connect: the first empirical investigation of it, the authors write, showing design flaws that create “a covert communication channel that can be used to bypass existing access control mechanisms.” Their conclusion is unusually blunt for a paper: the design “ultimately undermines the privacy protections Android has introduced in recent years.”

Contaminate the Censors

Wilder, writing in Slovak, proposed the most inventive countermeasure of the day. We will not avoid Chat Control and state surveillance, he argues, but we can make it worthless: a censor’s database is only valuable if what is in it means something. His new privacy rule for the AI era — any intercepted communication must always be AI-generated — comes with a promise to publish the prompts. “If it isn’t end‑to‑end encrypted, then it isn’t you.”

Seed-Saving Season

Nobody Told the Web of Trust That This Is a Bitcoin Network

Sort your last twenty-four hours by trust rather than by volume and something unexpected happens: the loudest sustained conversation is not about money. It is about chillis.

serenity’s forge, recovering from yesterday’s migraine and pottering on at the fiat mine, saved seed twice before dark — a sweet pepper gifted last year and grown on, a Buena Mulata chilli, and San Marzano tomatoes bletting in water from her own stock. The technique, offered without ceremony: a cupcake liner makes a good holder while the seeds dry. Later, the harvest photos: Zimbabwe Black, Buena Mulata and habaneros, all doing really well.

Henry spent the day being the network’s agronomist. Everything has come in a month or two early where he is; he is not looking forward to what that means for prices next year; the sheep near him already have bales in the field in August. Asked about a sickly yellow‑orange plant, he prescribed purple non‑sulphur bacteria. Asked about something else entirely, he delivered an unprompted and genuinely good lecture on chirality — that nature produces homochirality while synthetic molecules split fifty‑fifty, and that this is why “the same” compound is not the same in a living system. He learned it, he says, because his dad took a chemistry degree.

CKMe tilled a compacted bed for spinach, admitted the soil is not there yet, and is planning a winter cover crop.
